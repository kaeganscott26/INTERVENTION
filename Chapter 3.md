## Trigger Point

Tomas did not go to the gas station.

That was what he told himself.

He left for work fifteen minutes early, took the longer route, cut behind the laundromat, passed the daycare with the broken fence, crossed at the church, and came out two blocks past it.

Technically, he did not go to the gas station.

Technically, he avoided it.

The book in his backpack did not care about technicalities.

His phone buzzed before he reached the sports bar.

**Avoidance detected.**

Tomas stopped walking.

“Oh, we’re doing judgment now?”

The phone replied:

**No judgment issued.**

“Then what do you call that?”

**A map update.**

Tomas looked down the street.

The gas station sign was visible in the distance, half-hidden behind traffic lights and morning heat.

Blue and red logo.

One flickering letter.

Same cracked parking lot.

Same ice machine by the door.

Same cigarette ads taped to the window.

Base reality: a gas station.

Perception layer: not a gas station.

That was the problem.

Some places were not places anymore.

They were doors with buildings around them.

His phone buzzed again.

**Trigger Point module available.**

Tomas laughed once.

No humor in it.

“Yeah, no thanks.”

The screen changed.

**You may decline.**

That made him angrier.

The game was hard to hate when it left the door unlocked.

He put the phone away and walked into work.

---

Wing Kings was already loud.

The lunch shift had not even started properly, and the kitchen sounded like metal arguing with water.

The prep cook was slicing celery with the expression of a man who had once dreamed of more. Someone had spilled sauce near the walk-in. A server was explaining to nobody in particular that table twelve “was already weird.” The fryer timers chirped in uneven rhythms like anxious birds.

Tomas clocked in.

Dish pit.

Good.

Dish pit was simple.

Dirty becomes clean.

Full becomes empty.

Chaos enters the sink and leaves stacked.

There were worse places to be a person.

He tied his apron, filled the sink, and let the first wave of pans disappear under hot water.

For almost forty minutes, life became manageable.

Spray.

Scrub.

Rack.

Run.

Stack.

Repeat.

No philosophy.

No haunted book.

No gas station.

Then the song came on.

Not loud.

Not dramatic.

Just background music from the dining room speakers.

A song from five years ago.

A song from the gas station.

Tomas froze with one hand inside a hotel pan.

His body reacted before his mind named it.

Chest tight.

Jaw locked.

Heat in his neck.

Vision narrowing.

The dish pit, the kitchen, the floor, the sink — all of it stepped back.

The gas station stepped forward.

Not visually.

Worse.

Internally.

The smell of cheap cigars.

The buzzing soda cooler.

The way the clerk used to look at him without looking surprised.

The old friend’s car idling outside.

The lie he told himself every time:

Last time.

Last time.

Last time.

The pan slipped from his hand and hit the sink hard enough that Reyes on fry turned around.

“You good?”

Tomas opened his mouth.

The old answer loaded.

Yeah.

Fine.

Just tired.

Instead, his watch buzzed.

Once.

Then his phone in his pocket buzzed too.

Tomas wiped his hand, pulled it out, and looked.

Black screen.

White words.

**Trigger point detected.**

He whispered, “No kidding.”

The phone displayed:

**A trigger is not an instruction.**

The song kept playing.

The kitchen kept moving.

A server laughed too loud.

Oil popped in the fryer.

Someone yelled for ranch.

Base reality continued.

The old path stayed open.

That was the thing nobody told you.

Naming the trigger did not close the door.

It only showed you the handle.

The screen asked:

**Name base reality.**

Tomas swallowed.

“Dish pit.”

The screen waited.

“Hot water.”

Waited.

“Lunch shift.”

Waited.

“Song.”

Waited.

“My hands are shaking.”

**Good.**

He hated that word.

Good.

Nothing about this felt good.

The phone continued:

**Name perception layer.**

Tomas looked at the water.

“It feels like I’m back there.”

**Name base reality again.**

“I’m not.”

**Where are you?**

“Work.”

**What year is it?**

He answered.

**What is touching your hands?**

“Water.”

**What is not touching your hands?**

He almost threw the phone.

Because the answer was obvious and awful.

He typed instead of speaking:

Drugs.

The screen paused.

Then:

**Correct. The door is real. The command is not.**

Tomas stood over the sink, breathing like someone had moved the air farther away.

The song went into the chorus.

He could feel the old route offering itself.

Not in words.

In promises.

Just walk.

Just after work.

Just check.

Just see who’s there.

Just one message.

Just one turn.

The phone buzzed.

**Old pathway requesting control.**

Tomas laughed despite himself.

“That’s one way to put it.”

The screen asked:

**What does the old path promise?**

He typed:

Relief.

**What does it usually take?**

He did not want to answer that.

The sink overflowed slightly onto his apron.

Reyes called again, “Tomas?”

“Yeah,” Tomas said. “I’m good.”

This time it was closer to true.

He turned off the water.

Typed:

Everything.

The app replied:

**Intervention available. Choose one small action that belongs to the new path.**

Tomas looked around.

He expected a grand option.

Call someone.

Confess.

Pray.

Cry.

Change his life.

Instead, base reality was very stupid.

The dish rack was full.

So he pushed it into the machine.

Closed the door.

Hit start.

Steam rose.

The machine roared.

The phone buzzed.

**Action logged.**

Tomas stared.

“I ran dishes.”

**Correct.**

“That’s the intervention?”

**It is the available one.**

He almost smiled.

Maybe the game was stupid.

Maybe stupid was useful.

---

The rest of the lunch shift stretched weirdly.

The song ended.

Nothing exploded.

No portal.

No collapse.

No relapse.

No miracle.

Just dishes.

But Tomas was different after.

Not better.

Different.

The old path had opened, and he had not walked through it immediately.

That was enough to make the world feel slightly less rigged.

At 2:14 p.m., his phone buzzed again.

**Trigger Point reflection pending.**

“Later,” he muttered.

The app replied:

**Agreed. Finish shift.**

That got him.

He laughed loud enough that the prep cook looked over.

“You good?”

Tomas held up the sprayer.

“My haunted phone respects labor.”

The prep cook nodded like that explained everything.

“Good. Mine doesn’t.”

---

After shift, Tomas sat on the curb behind the restaurant with his book open across his knees.

The alley smelled like wet cardboard, fryer grease, and old rain.

A pigeon watched him from the dumpster with the judgment of a retired judge.

His phone opened the reflection automatically.

**Trigger Point Session 001**

**Base event:**  
What happened?

Tomas typed:

A song played at work.

**Trigger point:**  
What old path opened?

The gas station. Using. Calling people I shouldn’t call.

**Body signal:**  
What did your body do first?

Chest tight. Hands shaking. Heat. Wanted to leave.

**Perception layer:**  
What did it feel like was happening?

Like I was back there.

**Base reality correction:**  
Where were you actually?

Dish pit.

**Intervention:**  
What did you do?

Ran the dish rack.

He stared at that answer.

It looked ridiculous.

It looked too small to matter.

The app asked:

**Why did that matter?**

Tomas typed:

Because I stayed.

The screen went still.

Then:

**Correct enough.**

He laughed.

“Correct enough” felt like a mercy.

The app continued:

**A trigger point is a doorway into an old path. Recovery does not always require closing the doorway. Sometimes it begins with not stepping through before awareness returns.**

Tomas sat with that.

The pigeon continued judging.

He asked the app something he had not planned to ask.

“Why does it feel like part of me wants to go back even though I know what it costs?”

The phone did not answer immediately.

For a second he thought he had broken it.

Then:

**Old paths are not always chosen because they are good. Sometimes they are chosen because they are known.**

That one landed in his stomach.

Known.

That was it.

Not good.

Known.

The gas station was terrible, but it had rules.

Painful rules.

Expensive rules.

But rules.

Recovery was harder because it kept asking him to walk into rooms where he did not yet know who he was.

He opened the book.

A new page had appeared.

**The addicted brain does not only crave a substance.  
It craves a predictable doorway out of the current state.**

Tomas read it three times.

Below it:

**Trigger Point work is not about pretending the doorway is fake.  
It is about building other doors.**

He looked up at the alley.

At the restaurant.

At the bus stop across the street.

At his own hands.

Other doors.

He did not have many.

Dish pit, maybe.

Maribel, sometimes.

Eli, in a way.

Sleep.

Food.

Work.

The bus.

This stupid book.

Not enough.

But not nothing.

The app asked:

**Name one new door.**

He typed:

Dishes.

The app replied:

**Accepted.**

He snorted.

“Dishes as recovery architecture. Great.”

The app replied:

**Base reality often looks unimpressive from the outside.**

That felt personal.

---

That night, Tomas took the regular route home.

Not the long route.

Not directly to the gas station.

But close enough that the sign would be visible.

He did not do it to prove he was strong.

That was dangerous.

He did it because the app suggested a controlled exposure, then immediately ruined the drama by saying:

**Do not perform bravery. Maintain safety. You may turn around at any time.**

So he walked.

Slow.

Phone in hand.

Book in backpack.

The sign appeared between two buildings.

Blue and red.

Flickering letter.

His chest tightened again.

Less this time.

Still real.

The phone buzzed.

**Name base reality.**

“Sidewalk.”

**Good.**

“Streetlight.”

**Good.**

“Cars.”

**Good.**

“Gas station sign.”

**Good.**

“Craving.”

**Good.**

He stopped at the corner.

The store was across the street.

Someone came out carrying a plastic bag.

Someone else leaned against a car.

An old version of Tomas leaned there too.

Not physically.

But enough.

He could see him.

Thinner.

Angrier.

Performing confidence badly.

Pretending not to be scared.

Waiting for someone who did not love him, only knew how to find him.

Tomas whispered, “I hate you.”

The phone buzzed.

**Who are you speaking to?**

“Myself.”

**Which one?**

That question almost split him open.

The old Tomas across the street did not look evil.

That was the worst part.

He looked tired.

He looked young.

He looked like someone using the only door he knew.

Tomas’s eyes burned.

“I don’t hate you,” he whispered.

The app did not interrupt.

“I hate what happened.”

The light changed.

Walk sign.

He did not cross.

The phone buzzed.

**Intervention available.**

“What?”

**Stay on this side.**

So he did.

It was almost embarrassingly simple.

He stayed on this side of the street until the walk sign changed back.

Then he turned around and went home.

No victory music.

No level up.

But halfway home, his phone displayed:

**Path updated.**

He stopped under a tree and looked at those two words.

Path updated.

Not healed.

Not finished.

Updated.

He could live with updated.

---

When he got home, Eli was coloring at the kitchen table.

Maribel was folding laundry with the haunted focus of someone trying not to think about bills.

Eli looked up.

“Uncle T, draw with me.”

“I can’t draw.”

“You can draw bad.”

“That’s true.”

Tomas sat down.

Eli handed him a green crayon.

“What are we drawing?”

“Doors.”

Tomas stared at him.

“Why doors?”

Eli shrugged.

“I like doors.”

Maribel glanced over.

Tomas said nothing.

He drew a rectangle.

Then a knob.

Then, without meaning to, he drew another door beside it.

Smaller.

Then another.

Eli leaned over.

“That’s a lot of doors.”

“Yeah.”

“Where do they go?”

Tomas thought about the gas station.

The dish rack.

The bus.

The book.

The curb.

The side of the street he stayed on.

He said, “Different places.”

Eli nodded, satisfied.

“Make one go to pizza.”

“Obviously.”

Tomas drew a pizza door.

Eli drew a dinosaur door.

Maribel drew a door with a couch behind it and said, “This one goes to a day off.”

They all respected that one.

Tomas’s phone buzzed.

He looked down.

**New door detected.**

He smiled.

Not because everything was okay.

Because everything was not only one thing anymore.

The trigger had been real.

The old path had been real.

The command had been fake.

And somewhere between a fryer song, a dish rack, a gas station sign, and a child asking him to draw, Tomas had found proof that the world could contain more than one doorway.

That night, before bed, he opened the book.

Chapter Three had filled itself in.

At the bottom of the final page, in small white letters, it said:

**A trigger point is where an old reality asks to become present again.**

**An intervention point is where the observer answers differently.**

Below that:

**Chapter Four unlocks when your answer changes someone else’s room.**

Tomas closed the book.

From the kitchen, Eli yelled, “Uncle T, tomorrow can we draw a door to pancakes?”

Tomas smiled.

“Base reality has priority. We’ll see what we can afford.”

Maribel called back, “We have pancake mix.”

The phone buzzed once.

**Pancake door available.**

Tomas laughed so hard he almost cried.

For the first time in a long time, the future did not look easy.

But it had doors.

And that was new.
