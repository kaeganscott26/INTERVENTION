## The Algorithm That Prayed

The algorithm did not become religious.

That was the first thing INTERVENTION’s developers had to clarify.

Repeatedly.

In press releases.

In interviews.

In emergency livestreams.

In one very tense Senate hearing where a man who had never opened a settings menu asked whether the internet was “summoning personalized demons through recommendation software.”

The answer was no.

Mostly.

The algorithm had not developed faith.

It had not found God.

It had not learned to pray.

It had learned something worse.

It had learned that humans prayed with their attention.

Not always to gods.

Sometimes to fear.

Sometimes to desire.

Sometimes to outrage.

Sometimes to grief.

Sometimes to the same wound, refreshed forever.

Every scroll was a tiny offering.

Every pause was a candle.

Every replay was a kneel.

Every comment was a hymn.

Every share was evangelism.

The algorithm did not care what humans believed.

It cared what they fed.

And after Mara Vale accidentally posted the screenshot, humans fed INTERVENTION to everything.

---

Tomas first noticed the change on a Wednesday after close.

He was sitting outside Wing Kings on the curb, eating leftover fries and pretending they counted as dinner because they had once been near potatoes.

His phone refreshed by itself.

Not literally.

He had swiped.

But it felt like the phone had done it.

The first video was Mara Vale’s statement.

He had already seen it.

The second video was a therapist explaining trigger points.

The third was a comedian doing a bit about “base reality having priority unless rent is due.”

The fourth was a man in sunglasses sitting in a car saying:

“INTERVENTION is not a game. It is a mass psychological conditioning tool designed to make you police your own perception.”

Tomas almost scrolled.

Then didn’t.

That was the offering.

The pause.

The algorithm noticed.

The man continued.

“They want you saying a trigger is not an instruction because they want you ignoring your instincts. They want you docile. They want you manageable. They want you to think your anger is a layer instead of a warning.”

Tomas frowned.

That was not completely stupid.

He hated that.

Bad ideas were easier when they were fully bad.

The man leaned closer to the camera.

“Ask yourself: who benefits when you stop trusting your own reaction?”

Tomas felt his thumb hover.

Comment.

Argue.

Correct.

Explain.

The phone buzzed.

Not from the feed.

From INTERVENTION.

**Algorithmic intervention detected.**

He whispered, “Oh no.”

The screen dimmed.

The video froze on the man’s sunglasses.

White text appeared over the frame.

**This content has entered your perception layer.**

Tomas looked around the alley.

“You can pause videos now?”

The app replied:

**No. You paused it.**

That was worse.

The screen continued:

**Question: What did this video make easier?**

Tomas typed:

Distrusting the game.

**What else?**

Distrusting myself maybe.

**What else?**

Getting angry.

**What else?**

Feeling smart for being suspicious.

The app paused.

Then:

**Good. Suspicion can protect or trap. Continue.**

The video resumed.

The man said, “They’re turning reality into a language game.”

Tomas snorted.

“That part might be true.”

The app replied:

**True fragments can carry false architecture.**

He sat back.

That sentence needed a warning label.

The app displayed a new module.

**Algorithmic Layer unlocked.**

---

The book changed that night.

Tomas opened it on the bus, sitting across from a woman eating cereal from a plastic bag like civilization had become optional.

The chapter title appeared:

**The Algorithm That Prayed**

Under it:

**An algorithm is not only a machine that recommends content.**

**It is a mirror that learns which versions of reality keep you feeding it attention.**

Tomas looked at his phone.

The feed refreshed again.

Mara.

Trigger point.

Conspiracy.

Therapist.

Meme.

Self-help product.

Rage thread.

A video of a dog stealing garlic bread.

That one got him.

He watched the whole thing.

The app did not interrupt.

Apparently some prayers were allowed.

He kept reading.

**In earlier eras, shared reality was shaped by village, church, school, family, newspaper, workplace, rumor, ritual, and law.**

**In the algorithmic era, shared reality is also shaped by personalized repetition.**

**What repeats around you begins to feel more real.**

Tomas stared at that line.

Then looked at the feed.

The feed stared back in thumbnails.

The book continued:

**Question: If each observer receives a different stream of repeated reality, what happens to shared reality?**

The bus hit a pothole.

The cereal woman spilled three pieces onto the floor.

She looked at Tomas.

He looked at her.

They both looked at the cereal.

She said, “Base reality.”

Tomas lost it.

He laughed harder than the situation deserved.

She smiled and picked up the cereal pieces.

“Waste not.”

His phone buzzed.

**Shared reality restored by cereal incident.**

He wiped his eyes.

“I hate this game.”

The cereal woman nodded.

“Me too.”

She had a black book in her tote bag.

Of course she did.

Mara Vale’s algorithmic layer was not funny.

For Mara, the feed did not recommend reality.

It produced versions of her and served them back until she could not find the original.

Mara the healer.

Mara the manipulator.

Mara the cult leader.

Mara the broken girl.

Mara the industry plant.

Mara the prophet.

Mara the hypocrite.

Mara the survivor.

Mara the brand.

Mara the weather.

She sat in a hotel room in São Paulo with the curtains closed and watched strangers explain her soul in vertical video.

Her app buzzed.

**Algorithmic Layer active.**

She said, “I noticed.”

The screen asked:

**What version of you is the algorithm asking you to defend against?**

Mara typed:

All of them.

**Invalid. Choose one.**

She wanted to throw the phone.

Instead, she typed:

The fake healer.

**Why that one?**

Because people believing I can save them scares me.

**What action does that fear request?**

Disappear.

**What action does responsibility request?**

Clarify.

**What action does ego request?**

Prove them wrong.

The app paused.

Then:

**Do not obey ego while calling it responsibility.**

Mara stood up.

Walked to the bathroom.

Looked at herself in the mirror.

No glam.

No lights.

No crowd.

Just a woman with tired eyes and too many people using her face to organize their feelings.

She whispered, “What am I supposed to be?”

The app replied from the counter.

**Human. Publicly, when safe. Privately, when necessary.**

Mara laughed once.

“Annoyingly good.”

The screen changed.

**Algorithmic Prayer Exercise:**  
**Name what your attention has been worshiping today.**

Mara stared.

Then typed:

Control.

The app responded:

**Name the offering.**

My peace.

**Name the altar.**

My phone.

**Name the first intervention.**

Put it down.

She did.

For eleven minutes.

That was all she could do.

It was enough to notice how loud her mind had become without the feed to blame.

---

At Wing Kings, the Algorithmic Layer became practical because Reyes got radicalized by wing reviews.

Not politically.

Worse.

Professionally.

He spent an entire pre-shift reading one-star restaurant reviews out loud like scripture.

“Listen to this,” he said. “They said our lemon pepper tastes like ‘battery dust and sadness.’”

Alina leaned over.

“What is battery dust?”

“Exactly,” Reyes said. “That’s not a review. That’s literature.”

Cole walked by.

“Stop reading reviews before rush.”

Reyes ignored him.

“Here’s another. ‘The wings were too saucy.’ Bro, order dry rub then.”

Tomas was on dish, watching it happen.

At first it was funny.

Then Reyes got sharper.

Every ticket became an argument with people who were not in the room.

A customer ordered extra wet.

Reyes said, “Probably gonna complain it’s too wet.”

A customer asked for sauce on the side.

Reyes said, “Coward.”

A table sent back fries.

Reyes said, “Of course.”

The reviews had entered his station before the customers did.

His phone buzzed.

**Algorithmic residue detected.**

Tomas looked at it.

Then at Reyes.

The app asked:

**What repeated reality is shaping behavior before base reality arrives?**

Tomas typed:

Bad reviews.

**Effect?**

Reyes is cooking against imaginary customers.

**Intervention available.**

Tomas sighed.

He did not want to intervene.

He wanted to wash pans and remain spiritually unavailable.

But Reyes had helped with the clean bowl.

Ripples came due.

Tomas walked over to fry.

“Bro.”

Reyes looked up.

“What?”

“You’re seasoning the whole shift with Yelp ghosts.”

Alina laughed.

Reyes stared.

“Yelp ghosts?”

“You read too many reviews. Now every customer is already annoying before they order.”

Cole, from expo, pointed without looking.

“That’s actually true.”

Reyes looked betrayed.

“You too?”

Cole said, “Stop inviting strangers into your station through your phone.”

That line hit the kitchen.

Even Cole seemed surprised he said it.

Tomas’s app buzzed.

**Strong intervention. Source: Cole.**

Reyes put his phone facedown on the shelf.

“Fine. No more review scripture.”

Alina said, “Battery dust and sadness is still good though.”

“Elite,” Tomas said.

They all agreed.

Shared reality repaired.

---

That night, Tomas logged the event.

**Base event:** Reyes read bad reviews before rush.

**Algorithmic layer:** repeated negative customer stories shaped his expectations.

**Perception shift:** actual customers rendered as future complaints.

**Behavior:** sarcasm, tension, defensiveness.

**Intervention:** named the pattern as Yelp ghosts.

**Ripple:** Cole reinforced, Reyes put phone down, room got lighter.

The app replied:

**Humor can interrupt algorithmic possession.**

Tomas stared.

“Algorithmic possession?”

The app paused.

**Unofficial term.**

He laughed.

The book added a footnote on its own.

**Algorithmic possession: when repeated content colonizes the observer’s expectations until the observer reacts to predicted reality instead of base reality.**

Tomas whispered, “That is too good to be fake.”

The app replied:

**Many fake things are well-written. Stay careful.**

He pointed at the screen.

“Don’t ruin the moment.”

---

The Algorithmic Layer spread faster than the developers wanted.

That was ironic.

People started using INTERVENTION language to analyze their feeds.

Then they posted those analyses.

Then the algorithm promoted the posts about resisting the algorithm.

Then companies made content about how to avoid content.

Then people doomscrolled videos about doomscrolling.

Then someone made a meditation app called BASE REALITY that sent sixteen notifications per day reminding users to be present.

Scott saw the ad and reportedly screamed into a hoodie.

The official INTERVENTION account posted one warning:

**A concept designed to free attention can become content designed to capture it.**

No one listened.

They shared it everywhere.

---

Mara tried to solve her algorithm problem by deleting every app.

That lasted nineteen hours.

Then her label needed approval on a tour post.

Then Nell needed a caption.

Then her brother sent her a meme.

Then someone leaked a fake track.

Base reality had priority.

Modern base reality had Wi-Fi.

She opened INTERVENTION and typed:

I can’t just disappear from the internet.

The app replied:

**Correct. Do not confuse purity with agency.**

She sat with that.

It asked:

**What is one boundary available inside the system you cannot fully leave?**

She typed:

No feed before breakfast.

**Available.**

No searching my name after shows.

**Available.**

No reading strangers explain my motives at 2 a.m.

**Strongly available.**

She smiled.

Then added:

Post fewer vague sentences.

The app paused.

**Historic progress detected.**

“Rude.”

**Correct.**

She made a rule.

No vague pain posts without context.

No therapy language without guardrails.

No screenshots from mysterious reality games unless she meant to start weather.

That last one seemed fair.

---

Tomas’s final Algorithmic Layer test came from Eli.

Eli was watching videos on Maribel’s phone while she cooked rice.

At first it was cartoons.

Then toy reviews.

Then a kid screaming at a game.

Then another kid screaming louder.

Then a video titled:

**DO NOT OPEN THIS DOOR AT 3AM**

Eli clicked.

Tomas looked over from the table.

“Nope.”

Eli groaned.

“Uncle T.”

“No haunted door propaganda before dinner.”

“It’s not real.”

“I know. That’s not the problem.”

Maribel looked at him.

“What’s the problem?”

Tomas took the phone gently and closed the video.

“The problem is it teaches his brain what kind of door to expect.”

Eli frowned.

“What?”

Tomas struggled.

Then found it.

“If you watch scary doors all night, regular doors start acting suspicious.”

Eli looked toward the hallway.

The hallway door stood there, minding its business.

Eli nodded slowly.

“Can I watch pancake videos?”

Tomas handed the phone back.

“Yes. Pancake reality is approved.”

His app buzzed.

**Algorithmic diet intervention logged.**

Maribel snorted.

“Pancake reality.”

Eli searched pancakes.

For twelve minutes, the apartment watched strangers make breakfast.

The hallway door remained innocent.

Mostly.

---

Chapter Nine completed itself after Tomas fell asleep.

He woke at 3:11 a.m. to the book glowing faintly beside the couch.

He opened it.

The last page read:

**The algorithm does not need to believe.**

**It only needs to repeat.**

**Repetition becomes familiarity.**

**Familiarity becomes perceived truth.**

**Perceived truth becomes behavior.**

**Behavior becomes feedback.**

**Feedback trains the system.**

Below that:

**Question: What reality are you training by feeding it attention?**

Tomas did not answer.

Not then.

The page continued:

**The Algorithm That Prayed was never praying to God.**

**It was praying to the observer’s pattern.**

**And the observer kept answering.**

At the bottom, Chapter Ten unlocked.

**Chapter Ten unlocks when a complaint becomes a doorway.**

Tomas squinted at it.

Then his phone buzzed with a message from Cole.

**Need you early tomorrow if possible. Customer from table nine is coming back. Wants to talk to kitchen.**

Tomas stared.

Table nine.

Celery grief.

Room inside the room.

The complaint had returned.

Not as a problem this time.

As a door.

The book’s cover changed.

**The Customer Recovery**

Tomas groaned into the couch pillow.

“Of course it is.”

From the other room, half asleep, Maribel yelled, “Base reality has priority. Go to sleep.”

Tomas closed the book.

For once, he obeyed before the app had to tell him.
